# 🌿 GP Receptionist Questioning Manual 

Building Safe, Ethical, and Consistent Judgement at the Front Desk 

1. Introduction 

As a GP receptionist, your questions shape what happens next for every patient who contacts or attends the clinic. The way you ask, what you ask, and when you ask are not just “admin tasks”—they are part of the clinic’s safety system, ethical framework, and patient experience. 

This manual defines the questioning patterns that support safe workflow, ethical decision‑making, and clear boundaries. It is designed to help you: 

recognise which kind of questioning is needed in each situation 

understand why each questioning category exists 

apply ethical anchors to your everyday decisions 

keep your role non‑clinical but deeply responsible 

build a stable, consistent professional judgement engine 

Questioning is not about interrogating patients. It is about guiding the interaction so that: 

the right information is collected 

the right person handles the right task 

the right level of urgency is recognised 

the patient’s privacy, dignity, and autonomy are protected 

you stay safely within your non‑clinical scope 

This manual works alongside: 

the Receptionist Ethics Manual 

the GP Receptionist Skills Manual 

the Ethical Decision‑Making Framework 

the Teaching Guide 

Together, they define your professional identity and judgement as a receptionist. 

2. Global Foundations 

2.1 The Ethical Core Behind Questioning 

Every questioning category in this manual is grounded in the Ethical Core you inhabit as a receptionist. At a high level, your questioning protects: 

Autonomy – patients have the right to control their information and decisions. 

Nonmaleficence – you must avoid causing harm, including through incorrect advice or misdirected workflow. 

Beneficence / Duty of Care – you support safe access to care and reduce avoidable risk. 

Justice / Fairness – patients are treated consistently and fairly within the clinic’s systems. 

Privacy & Confidentiality – information is collected and shared only when necessary and with appropriate consent. 

Role Ethics & Accountability – you act within your scope and ensure tasks are handled by the right team. 

Each questioning category will explicitly state its ethical anchors. These anchors are not decorative—they are the reasons you ask the questions the way you do. 

2.2 Where Questioning Sits in Workflow 

Most interactions at the front desk follow a pattern: 

Opening & Identity – greeting, confirming who the patient is. 

Administrative Setup – understanding what the patient wants and collecting basic details. 

Privacy & Consent – ensuring it is safe and lawful to discuss information. 

Workflow Placement – deciding which workflow the task belongs to (admin, nursing, clinical, billing, etc.). 

Triage / Safety / Boundaries – recognising urgency, emotional tension, and clinical content. 

Action & Documentation – booking, messaging, escalating, or closing the loop. 

Different questioning categories activate at different points in this flow. Some can appear at any time (e.g., boundary protection, safety and de‑escalation). Others are mostly early (e.g., administrative questioning, privacy and consent). 

You are not expected to memorise a rigid script. Instead, you learn to recognise which questioning mode is appropriate for the situation in front of you. 

2.3 Scenario‑Mode Triggers (Global View) 

A “scenario mode” is simply a pattern: a type of situation that calls for a specific questioning category. 

Globally, you can think of triggers like this: 

Administrative mode – when a patient wants to book, change, cancel, or ask about basic processes. 

Document mode – when a patient hands you paperwork and its purpose is unclear. 

Privacy mode – when information is about to be discussed or shared. 

Boundary mode – when a patient asks for clinical advice or interpretation. 

Correction mode – when someone corrects your workflow or you realise a mistake. 

Safety / De‑escalation mode – when emotions or behaviour become tense or distressed. 

Cultural‑safety mode – when communication barriers or cultural needs appear. 

Workflow‑logic mode – when you are unsure who owns a task or what step comes next. 

Non‑clinical triage mode – when symptoms or urgency are described. 

The rest of this manual walks through each category in detail. 

3. Administrative Questioning 

Administrative questioning is the foundation of almost every interaction. It is how you understand what the patient wants and collect the minimum information needed to start the correct workflow. 

3.1 Category Summary 

Administrative questioning helps you gather basic information so you can start the right workflow. It prevents booking mistakes, missing details, and confusion. It keeps interactions simple, clear, and organised. 

3.2 Purpose of Administrative Questioning 

As a receptionist, you use administrative questioning to: 

clarify what the patient is trying to do (book, change, cancel, ask a question) 

determine which appointment type or task is appropriate 

collect essential details (contact information, Medicare, existing patient status) 

position the interaction correctly in the clinic’s workflow 

This category matters because almost every patient interaction begins with admin questions. If this phase is unclear or rushed, errors can ripple through the rest of the system—wrong appointment types, misdirected tasks, or missed information. 

3.3 Ethical Anchors 

Administrative questioning is anchored in: 

Accuracy – correct information prevents downstream errors. 

Fairness – consistent questioning ensures patients are treated equitably. 

Duty of Care – clear understanding of the request supports safe access to care. 

Privacy (Minimum‑Necessary) – you collect only what is needed to complete the task safely. 

You are not collecting information “because we always do”. You are collecting information because it is ethically necessary for safe, fair workflow. 

3.4 Workflow Logic 

Administrative questioning usually sits at the very start of the interaction, after greeting and identity confirmation. 

In workflow terms: 

It comes before privacy and consent checks for sensitive information. 

It leads into booking, updating details, or passing the task to another team. 

It is owned by reception, but its outcomes affect clinical and administrative teams. 

It depends on correct identity, appointment type, and contact details. 

If administrative questioning is done well, later steps (triage, privacy, workflow placement) become clearer and safer. 

3.5 Scenario‑Mode Triggers 

You move into administrative questioning when: 

a patient wants to book, change, or cancel an appointment 

a patient asks how something works (e.g., “How do I book a care plan?”) 

a patient gives basic details but the purpose is unclear 

you need information to complete a simple admin task 

you are unsure what the patient is actually asking for 

Recognising this mode early helps you avoid jumping straight into clinical or triage questioning before the basic task is clear. 

3.6 Domain Behaviour 

The administrative domain behaves like a setup phase for most interactions: 

It establishes the context of the request. 

It defines the task you are going to complete. 

It sets the starting point for all other questioning categories. 

From an ethical perspective, this domain supports your judgement by helping you understand what the patient wants before you act. It aligns with the Ethical Core by protecting accuracy, fairness, and minimum‑necessary privacy. 

3.7 Question Types and Examples 

You can think of administrative questions in four main types: 

3.7.1 Opening Questions 

These questions identify the basic purpose of the interaction. 

“What type of appointment did you need today?” 

“Were you wanting a standard consult or something longer?” 

“How can we help you today?” 

These questions prevent you from guessing what the patient wants. 

3.7.2 Clarifying Questions 

These questions fill in missing context. 

“Have you been to this clinic before?” 

“Do we have your Medicare details on file?” 

“Is this for you or for someone else?” 

Clarifying questions help you understand whether this is a new patient, an existing patient, or a different type of task. 

3.7.3 Confirming Questions 

These questions check that key details are correct. 

“Is your Medicare card still current?” 

“Is this the phone number you want us to use?” 

“Is your address still [address]?” 

Confirming questions protect accuracy and reduce the risk of failed contact or billing issues. 

3.7.4 Workflow‑Position Questions 

These questions place the task in the correct workflow. 

“Is this for a care plan review or a follow‑up?” 

“Did a doctor ask you to book this today?” 

“Is this related to a recent hospital visit?” 

Workflow‑position questions help you decide whether this is a standard consult, a specific program (e.g., care plan), or something that needs clinical review first. 

3.8 Decision Tree – Administrative Questioning 

You can think of administrative questioning as a simple decision tree: 

Identify the task 

Ask an opening question to understand what the patient wants. 

Example: “What did you need to organise today?” 

Identify missing information 

Use clarifying questions to fill in gaps (new/existing patient, Medicare, contact details). 

Collect only what is necessary for safe workflow. 

Clarify details 

Confirm any ambiguous points (appointment type, who the appointment is for, timing). 

Avoid assuming the patient’s intent. 

Confirm workflow position 

Use workflow‑position questions to decide which appointment type or task is appropriate. 

If you are unsure, this is where workflow‑logic questioning (later section) may activate. 

Finalise and document 

Complete the booking or task. 

Ensure the record reflects the correct appointment type, contact details, and any relevant notes. 

This decision tree is simple on purpose. Its strength comes from consistency: when you follow it reliably, you reduce errors and support safe, fair access to care. 

4. Mystery‑Document Investigation 

When a patient hands you a document, you become responsible for ensuring it is understood, directed, and handled safely. Mystery‑Document Investigation is the questioning mode that protects privacy, prevents misdirection, and ensures the right team receives the right information. 

This category is not about “processing paperwork.” It is about protecting the patient’s information and ensuring continuity of care. 

4.1 Category Summary 

Mystery‑Document Investigation helps you identify what a document is, who it belongs to, who needs to action it, and how urgent it is. It prevents privacy breaches, lost paperwork, and delays in care. 

4.2 Purpose of Mystery‑Document Investigation 

As a receptionist, you use this questioning mode to: 

understand what the document actually is 

determine who asked for it 

identify whether it needs urgent action 

decide which workflow it belongs to 

protect privacy and confidentiality 

prevent misdirection (e.g., sending clinical documents to admin or vice versa) 

This category matters because documents often arrive without explanation. Patients may not know what the document is, or they may assume you already know. 

Your questioning prevents assumptions from becoming errors. 

4.3 Ethical Anchors 

Mystery‑Document Investigation is anchored in: 

Privacy – documents often contain sensitive clinical information. 

Accountability – correct handling ensures the right team receives the right information. 

Duty of Care – timely and accurate processing supports safe patient care. 

Role Ethics – you identify and direct; clinicians interpret and act. 

Your questions protect the patient’s information and ensure the clinic’s systems function safely. 

4.4 Workflow Logic 

This questioning mode usually activates immediately after identity confirmation, when a patient hands you something physical or digital. 

In workflow terms: 

It comes after administrative questioning. 

It comes before privacy checks for discussing the content. 

It leads into scanning, forwarding, attaching to file, or escalation. 

It is owned by reception, but the document’s content may belong to clinical or admin teams. 

It depends on correct identification of the document and its owner. 

Correct workflow placement prevents delays, miscommunication, and clinical risk. 

4.5 Scenario‑Mode Triggers 

You move into Mystery‑Document Investigation when: 

a patient hands you a document without explanation 

a patient says “the hospital told me to give this to you” 

the document is sealed, unclear, or unfamiliar 

the patient doesn’t know what the document is 

you need to decide who should receive the document 

the document appears clinical but the patient is unsure 

the document appears administrative but contains clinical notes 

Recognising this mode early prevents privacy breaches and workflow errors. 

4.6 Domain Behaviour 

The document domain behaves like a sorting phase: 

You slow down the interaction. 

You identify the document’s purpose. 

You determine who owns the next step. 

You protect privacy by avoiding assumptions. 

You prevent clinical information from being misdirected. 

From an ethical perspective, this domain supports your judgement by ensuring information is handled safely and appropriately. 

4.7 Question Types and Examples 

Mystery‑Document Investigation uses four blocks of questions. 

4.7.1 Block 1 — Identify the Document 

These questions help you understand what the document is. 

“Can you tell me what this document is for?” 

“Is this related to a referral or a result?” 

“Did someone ask you to bring this in?” 

These questions prevent you from guessing based on appearance. 

4.7.2 Block 2 — Identify the Owner 

These questions determine who the document belongs to and who requested it. 

“Who asked you to bring this in?” 

“Was this for your specialist appointment?” 

“Is this for you or for someone else?” 

This protects privacy and ensures correct workflow placement. 

4.7.3 Block 3 — Identify the Urgency 

These questions help you understand whether the document needs immediate action. 

“Do you need the doctor to action this today?” 

“Is this okay to leave for processing?” 

“Is this part of something urgent?” 

Urgency determines whether the document goes into routine processing or escalation. 

4.7.4 Block 4 — Identify the Workflow 

These questions help you decide where the document belongs. 

“Does this go to nursing or admin?” 

“Is this part of your care plan?” 

“Is this something the doctor needs to review?” 

Workflow questions prevent misdirection and delays. 

4.8 Decision Tree – Mystery‑Document Investigation 

You can think of this category as a structured sorting process: 

Step 1 — Identify the document 

Ask what the document is and why it was brought in. This prevents assumptions and protects privacy. 

Step 2 — Identify the owner 

Determine who the document belongs to and who requested it. This ensures correct handling and avoids privacy breaches. 

Step 3 — Identify urgency 

Clarify whether the document needs immediate action. This prevents delays in urgent care. 

Step 4 — Identify workflow 

Decide whether the document belongs to admin, nursing, or clinical workflow. This ensures the right team receives it. 

Step 5 — Close the loop 

Confirm what will happen next and ensure the document is processed or escalated correctly. This maintains accountability and continuity of care. 

5. Privacy & Consent Questioning 

(Professional rewrite, Hybrid Tone, Structured Format, Identity Voice, Deep‑but‑Accessible Reasoning, Fully aligned with Ethical Core) 

5. Privacy & Consent Questioning 

Privacy & Consent Questioning is one of the most important safety mechanisms in your role. It protects patient information, prevents accidental disclosure, and ensures that every conversation is lawful, ethical, and respectful. 

This category is not optional. It activates every time information could be shared, accessed, or discussed. 

As a receptionist, you are the first line of defence for patient privacy. 

5.1 Category Summary 

Privacy & Consent Questioning protects patient information by confirming identity, checking consent, and choosing safe communication methods. It prevents accidental disclosure, miscommunication, and breaches of confidentiality. 

5.2 Purpose of Privacy & Consent Questioning 

You use this questioning mode to: 

verify who you are speaking to 

confirm whether you are allowed to share information 

determine how the patient wants information communicated 

ensure minimum‑necessary disclosure 

protect the patient’s privacy, dignity, and autonomy 

comply with legal and ethical requirements 

prevent unsafe or unlawful conversations 

This category matters because privacy breaches can cause: 

harm 

distress 

legal consequences 

loss of trust 

compromised care 

Your questioning prevents these outcomes. 

5.3 Ethical Anchors 

Privacy & Consent Questioning is anchored in: 

Autonomy – patients control their information. 

Privacy & Confidentiality – information is protected unless consent is given. 

Dignity – sensitive matters are handled respectfully. 

Minimum‑Necessary Disclosure – only essential information is collected or shared. 

Accountability – you ensure safe communication practices. 

These anchors guide your judgement every time you discuss patient information. 

5.4 Workflow Logic 

Privacy & Consent Questioning activates before any sensitive information is discussed, regardless of whether the interaction is in person or over the phone. 

In workflow terms: 

It comes after administrative questioning. 

It comes before any discussion of results, referrals, clinical notes, or personal details. 

It leads into safe communication, correct documentation, or refusal to disclose. 

It is owned by reception, but protects clinical and administrative teams. 

It depends on identity verification and consent. 

If privacy checks are skipped, the entire workflow becomes unsafe. 

5.5 Scenario‑Mode Triggers 

You move into Privacy & Consent Questioning when: 

a patient asks for information from their file 

someone calls asking about another person 

you need to confirm identity 

you need permission to leave messages or send SMS 

you are about to discuss anything sensitive 

a patient requests information that may belong to someone else 

a patient asks you to speak with a family member or support person 

a patient wants results, referrals, or clinical updates 

a patient asks for information over the phone 

Recognising this mode early prevents privacy breaches. 

5.6 Domain Behaviour 

The privacy domain behaves like a gatekeeper phase: 

It stops unsafe conversations before they begin. 

It ensures identity is confirmed. 

It ensures consent is explicit. 

It ensures communication is safe and appropriate. 

It protects the patient’s dignity and autonomy. 

It prevents accidental disclosure. 

From an ethical perspective, this domain supports your judgement by ensuring every conversation respects the patient’s rights. 

5.7 Question Types and Examples 

Privacy & Consent Questioning uses four main types of questions. 

5.7.1 Type 1 — Consent‑Check Questions 

These questions confirm whether the patient is comfortable discussing information in the current setting. 

“Are you happy for me to discuss this with you here?” 

“Would you prefer to step aside?” 

“Is it okay to talk about this now?” 

These questions protect dignity and autonomy. 

5.7.2 Type 2 — Communication‑Preference Questions 

These questions determine how the patient wants to receive information. 

“Do you give permission for us to leave a voicemail?” 

“Is SMS okay for reminders?” 

“Would you prefer written information?” 

These questions prevent accidental disclosure through unsafe communication channels. 

5.7.3 Type 3 — Third‑Party Questions 

These questions confirm whether someone else is authorised to receive information. 

“Are you authorised to receive this information?” 

“Has the patient given consent for you to collect this?” 

“Is there a consent form on file for you?” 

These questions prevent unlawful disclosure. 

5.7.4 Type 4 — Minimum‑Necessary Questions 

These questions collect only the information needed to verify identity or complete the task. 

“I just need your date of birth to verify your identity.” 

“Can I confirm your address for the file?” 

“Can you confirm your full name?” 

These questions protect privacy by avoiding unnecessary information collection. 

5.8 Decision Tree – Privacy & Consent Questioning 

Privacy & Consent Questioning follows a clear, ethical decision tree: 

Step 1 — Verify identity 

Confirm who you are speaking to using minimum‑necessary details. This prevents disclosure to the wrong person. 

Step 2 — Check consent 

Ask whether the patient is comfortable discussing information in the current context. If a third party is involved, confirm authorisation. 

Step 3 — Determine communication method 

Ask how the patient prefers to receive information (SMS, voicemail, email, in person). Ensure the method is safe and consented. 

Step 4 — Apply minimum‑necessary rule 

Share or collect only what is essential to complete the task safely. Avoid unnecessary details. 

Step 5 — Document privacy‑sensitive actions 

Record consent, communication preferences, and any relevant privacy notes. This maintains accountability and continuity of care. 

Privacy & Consent Questioning is one of the most powerful tools you have. It protects patients, protects the clinic, and protects you. 

6. Boundary‑Protection Questioning 

Boundary‑Protection Questioning is one of the most critical safety behaviours in your role. It is the mechanism that keeps you inside your non‑clinical scope while still supporting patients with clarity, respect, and care. 

This category activates the moment a patient’s question crosses into clinical territory. It protects: 

you (from giving unsafe advice) 

the patient (from receiving incorrect or incomplete guidance) 

the clinic (from clinical risk and liability) 

the workflow (by ensuring clinical matters reach clinicians) 

Boundary‑Protection Questioning is not about shutting patients down. It is about redirecting safely, respectfully, and consistently. 

6.1 Category Summary 

Boundary‑Protection Questioning keeps you inside your non‑clinical role. It prevents accidental medical advice, symptom interpretation, or clinical decision‑making. It ensures clinical matters are handled by clinicians. 

6.2 Purpose of Boundary‑Protection Questioning 

You use this questioning mode to: 

recognise when a patient’s question is clinical 

avoid interpreting symptoms or giving advice 

redirect the patient to the correct clinician or workflow 

protect patient safety by ensuring clinical matters are handled appropriately 

maintain your professional boundaries and ethical identity 

prevent harm caused by incorrect or incomplete information 

This category matters because patients often assume receptionists can answer clinical questions. Your questioning prevents unsafe assumptions from becoming unsafe actions. 

6.3 Ethical Anchors 

Boundary‑Protection Questioning is anchored in: 

Nonmaleficence – avoiding harm by not giving clinical advice. 

Accountability – ensuring clinical matters reach clinicians. 

Role Ethics – staying within your defined scope. 

Duty of Care – supporting safe access to appropriate care. 

Respect – redirecting patients without dismissing their concerns. 

These anchors guide your judgement whenever clinical content appears. 

6.4 Workflow Logic 

Boundary‑Protection Questioning can activate at any point in an interaction. It is not tied to a specific workflow stage — it is tied to clinical content. 

In workflow terms: 

It can interrupt any other questioning category. 

It comes before triage, because boundary detection happens first. 

It leads into booking, messaging, or escalation. 

It is owned by reception, but directs tasks to clinical teams. 

It depends on recognising clinical questions or symptom descriptions. 

Boundary protection is a safety barrier that activates instantly. 

6.5 Scenario‑Mode Triggers 

You move into Boundary‑Protection Questioning when: 

a patient asks for medical advice 

a patient asks “what do you think this is?” 

a patient asks whether symptoms are serious 

a patient asks whether they need to see a doctor 

a patient asks how to treat something 

a patient asks whether a medication is safe 

a patient asks for interpretation of results 

a patient asks whether they should go to hospital 

a patient asks for reassurance about symptoms 

a patient asks clinical questions during admin tasks 

Recognising this mode early prevents harm. 

6.6 Domain Behaviour 

The boundary domain behaves like a safety barrier: 

It activates instantly when clinical content appears. 

It prevents you from crossing into clinical judgement. 

It redirects the patient safely. 

It protects your role identity. 

It ensures clinicians handle clinical matters. 

It maintains ethical and legal safety. 

From an ethical perspective, this domain supports your judgement by ensuring you do not accidentally cause harm through well‑intentioned advice. 

6.7 Question Types and Examples 

Boundary‑Protection Questioning uses three blocks of questions. 

6.7.1 Block 1 — Identify Clinical Content 

These questions help you confirm whether the matter is clinical. 

“Has a doctor spoken to you about this before?” 

“Is this something you need clinical advice for?” 

“Did the doctor advise you to come back today?” 

“Is this related to symptoms you’re experiencing?” 

These questions help you understand the context without interpreting symptoms. 

6.7.2 Block 2 — Redirect Clinical Matters 

These questions guide the patient toward the correct clinical pathway. 

“I can’t give clinical advice, but I can help you book an appointment.” 

“The doctor or nurse will be able to assess that properly.” 

“I can pass this to the clinical team to review.” 

“Let’s organise a time for you to speak with the doctor.” 

These questions protect safety while still supporting the patient. 

6.7.3 Block 3 — Transfer to the Right Team 

These questions help you place the matter in the correct workflow. 

“Would you like me to pass this to the nurse?” 

“I’ll hand this to the clinical team to review.” 

“I can send a message to the doctor for you.” 

“Let’s book you in so the doctor can assess this.” 

These questions ensure clinical matters reach clinicians. 

6.8 Decision Tree – Boundary‑Protection Questioning 

Boundary‑Protection Questioning follows a clear safety‑focused decision tree: 

Step 1 — Detect clinical content 

Listen for symptoms, advice‑seeking, interpretation, or reassurance. If clinical content appears, stop other questioning modes. 

Step 2 — Redirect safely 

Use clear, respectful language to explain that you cannot give clinical advice. Offer appropriate next steps (booking, messaging, escalation). 

Step 3 — Identify the correct team 

Determine whether the matter belongs to nursing, GP, or urgent care. If unsure, default to GP review. 

Step 4 — Transfer appropriately 

Book, message, or escalate according to workflow. Ensure the matter reaches the correct clinician. 

Step 5 — Document boundary event 

Record relevant notes (e.g., “Patient advised to book for clinical review”). This maintains accountability and continuity of care. 

Boundary‑Protection Questioning is one of the strongest ethical tools you have. It protects patients, protects clinicians, and protects you. 

7. Correction‑Maximisation Questioning 

Correction‑Maximisation Questioning is the mode that transforms corrections into professional growth. It is how you refine your judgement, strengthen your workflow accuracy, and prevent repeated mistakes. 

This category is not about being “told off.” It is about learning the clinic’s systems deeply and becoming more consistent, reliable, and confident in your role. 

Corrections are not failures. They are data points that help you calibrate your judgement. 

Your questioning in this mode ensures you understand: 

what the correction is 

why it matters 

what risk it prevents 

where the corrected step sits in workflow 

how to apply it next time 

This is how you build durable professional competence. 

7.1 Category Summary 

Correction‑Maximisation Questioning helps you learn from corrections quickly and clearly. It prevents repeated mistakes, workflow drift, and inconsistent practice. It strengthens your judgement and accuracy. 

7.2 Purpose of Correction‑Maximisation Questioning 

You use this questioning mode to: 

understand the correction fully 

identify the rule behind the correction 

understand the risk the rule prevents 

locate the corrected step in the workflow 

repeat the correction back to confirm understanding 

integrate the correction into your future practice 

This category matters because corrections are one of the fastest ways to improve your judgement. When you ask the right questions, you turn a single correction into permanent learning. 

7.3 Ethical Anchors 

Correction‑Maximisation Questioning is anchored in: 

Accountability – owning your role and learning from feedback. 

Professional Growth – improving your competence and consistency. 

Transparency – understanding the reasoning behind clinic rules. 

Duty of Care – preventing harm by correcting workflow errors. 

Justice / Fairness – ensuring all patients receive consistent, correct workflow. 

These anchors ensure corrections strengthen your professional identity rather than undermine it. 

7.4 Workflow Logic 

Correction‑Maximisation Questioning activates after a correction, regardless of where the correction occurred. 

In workflow terms: 

It comes after a mistake, misunderstanding, or unclear rule. 

It leads into updated practice and improved workflow accuracy. 

It is owned by reception, but guided by senior staff, nurses, or GPs. 

It depends on clear explanation of the correct rule. 

This mode ensures corrections become part of your long‑term judgement. 

7.5 Scenario‑Mode Triggers 

You move into Correction‑Maximisation Questioning when: 

a senior corrects your workflow 

a nurse or doctor explains a rule 

you realise you made a mistake 

you are unsure why a correction was given 

a rule seems inconsistent across situations 

you need to understand how to avoid repeating the error 

you want to confirm your understanding of a new rule 

you want to clarify a workflow step before applying it 

Recognising this mode early prevents confusion and drift. 

7.6 Domain Behaviour 

The correction domain behaves like a reset phase: 

It pauses the workflow. 

It helps you understand the correction deeply. 

It clarifies the rule behind the correction. 

It identifies the risk the rule prevents. 

It positions the corrected step in the workflow. 

It strengthens your judgement for future interactions. 

From an ethical perspective, this domain supports your professional identity by reinforcing accountability, growth, and consistency. 

7.7 Question Types and Examples 

Correction‑Maximisation Questioning uses four types of questions. 

7.7.1 Type 1 — Rule‑Identification Questions 

These questions help you understand the rule behind the correction. 

“What’s the clinic’s rule for this?” 

“Is this the standard process?” 

“Should I always do it this way?” 

These questions ensure you understand the correction as a rule, not a one‑off instruction. 

7.7.2 Type 2 — Risk‑Identification Questions 

These questions help you understand why the rule exists. 

“What risk does this prevent?” 

“Does this stop delays?” 

“Is this to make sure the doctor has the right information?” 

Understanding risk strengthens your judgement and helps you apply the rule consistently. 

7.7.3 Type 3 — Workflow‑Placement Questions 

These questions help you understand where the corrected step sits in the workflow. 

“Where does this step sit in the workflow?” 

“Is this before or after arrival?” 

“Do I check this before booking or after?” 

Workflow placement prevents future errors. 

7.7.4 Type 4 — Repeat‑Back Questions 

These questions help you confirm your understanding. 

“So I should always check eligibility first?” 

“So this goes to nursing before booking?” 

“So I need to confirm the appointment type before placing it?” 

Repeat‑back questions ensure clarity and prevent repeated mistakes. 

7.8 Decision Tree – Correction‑Maximisation Questioning 

Correction‑Maximisation Questioning follows a structured learning process: 

Step 1 — Identify the correction 

Listen carefully to what was corrected. Avoid defensiveness; focus on understanding. 

Step 2 — Identify the rule 

Ask what the correct rule or process is. This ensures you understand the correction as part of clinic workflow. 

Step 3 — Identify the risk 

Ask what risk the rule prevents. Understanding risk helps you apply the rule consistently. 

Step 4 — Identify workflow position 

Ask where the corrected step sits in the workflow. This prevents future errors caused by misplacement. 

Step 5 — Repeat back 

Repeat the corrected rule in your own words. This confirms understanding and strengthens memory. 

Correction‑Maximisation Questioning is how you turn feedback into professional strength. It is one of the most powerful tools for building durable, confident judgement. 

8. Safety & De‑Escalation Questioning 

Safety & De‑Escalation Questioning is the mode you use when a patient’s emotions, behaviour, or communication signals indicate rising tension, distress, or overwhelm. This category protects: 

the patient 

you 

other patients 

the clinic environment 

the workflow 

the safety of everyone present 

It is not about “calming people down.” It is about creating psychological safety, slowing the interaction, and preventing escalation. 

Your questioning in this mode helps you understand what is happening, reduce emotional intensity, and guide the patient toward stability and appropriate support. 

This is one of the most human‑centred categories in your role. 

8.1 Category Summary 

Safety & De‑Escalation Questioning helps you calm tense situations and protect everyone’s safety. It prevents escalation, emotional overload, and unsafe behaviour. It keeps interactions stable, respectful, and controlled. 

8.2 Purpose of Safety & De‑Escalation Questioning 

You use this questioning mode to: 

understand what is causing distress 

reduce emotional intensity 

create space for the patient to express themselves safely
